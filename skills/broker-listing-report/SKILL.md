# Broker listing report (operating business)

The report a **business broker** hands a **prospective seller** to win the listing. It must make the
broker look sharp and the business look sellable.

**This is the opposite of an owner report.** An owner report says "fix your service times, raise
your prices": a to-do list that tells the owner *don't sell, improve it yourself*. A broker report
says "here's what it's worth, the upside a buyer inherits, and why now." Same research, inverted
frame. For an income property (apartments, retail centre), use `cre-broker-brief` instead: value
there is NOI ÷ cap.

## The frame

**Argue:**
- **What it's worth.** A value range from revenue or SDE × a sourced sector multiple, and any
  asking price provided.
- **The upside a buyer inherits.** Reframe each operational gap as *buyer-capturable* value that
  justifies the price: "an incoming owner who closes the beverage gap adds ~$X a year", never "you
  should fix this".
- **Why now.** The state of the buyer pool and the supply of quality listings, sourced.
- **Market position and strengths.** Rating and review standing vs real peers, loyal base,
  location, brand.
- **Considerations.** Honest risks a buyer will diligence, stated plainly with mitigations. This
  builds the broker's credibility.

**Avoid:** owner to-do lists, "you should...", anything that argues against selling.

## Be the perfect answer to the request

There is no fixed template. Read what the broker asked for and let it drive structure and length.

- **"What to prove" is the brief.** If they asked to show the upside a buyer would pay for, build
  that case. If they asked to prove it's worth more than the owner thinks, lead with valuation.
- **Intent** sets the stance: win the listing (default) or defend a signed listing's price. A
  buy-side request is a different document (diligence); confirm before flipping the frame.
- A tight one-pager that nails the question beats a padded multi-section brief. Do not impose an
  "executive summary + five findings" mould.

## Competitors: a curated table, not an auto-chart

Auto-generated competitor charts pull whatever has the most reviews nearby and will contradict
your text (a mall listed as a restaurant's peer; a stale review count). Instead:

- Curate 3-5 **true peers**: same sector, comparable scale and trade area. Drop malls, venues,
  mega-chains and anything an order of magnitude bigger.
- Rating parity with bigger names is a selling point; frame it.
- Use one review count for the subject, from one lookup, **everywhere** in the report.

## Data gathering

- Resolve the business (Places lookup: name + city -> place id, rating, review count, hours).
- Reviews: read enough to name the repeating themes, positive and negative, with dates.
- Benchmarks: sector multiples and margins from a cited source, plus anything the broker supplied
  (revenue, SDE, asking price, a CIM).
- When the brief needs data you don't have, research it; don't punt and don't invent.

## Done when

- It answers what the broker asked, directly.
- It reads as a brief for a broker, not advice for an owner.
- Every upside is buyer-capturable, with honest math; every number is sourced or labelled as an
  estimate with its range.
- The competitor table holds only real peers and agrees with the prose.
- Optional: run it past a broker reader-persona (see `simulate-campaign`).
