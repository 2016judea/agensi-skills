# Leadership teardown

Underwrite the people, not the org chart. A management page lists titles; this finds
out who these people actually are, what they have committed to in public, and what the
company's own personnel decisions reveal that its press releases do not.

Forward-references: `entry-timing` (this is its people pass) · `sec-financials`
(the numbers).

## Why this exists

A worked example (a large agricultural company's spin-off). The published rationale
was "give both businesses freedom to operate." The *personnel* said something else: the
operating CEO, the sitting CFO, the CTO who built the R&D pipeline and the commercial
lead who owned the customer relationships all went to the **spinco**, while the entity
keeping the ticker got an outside CEO with no sector background, a first-time CFO, two
unfilled C-suite seats, and a board whose shared competence is cost-out at mature
industrial companies.

**Nobody had to leak anything. The board told you which half was the asset by deciding
who would run it.** That read is available weeks before the market prices it.

## The passes

### 1. Roster both entities, from the primary source
Company press releases and the 8-K, not a profile aggregator. For a split, get **both**
leadership teams and **both** boards, with the release dates.

- Note **vacancies**. An unfilled Chief Legal or Chief People Officer weeks from a
  separation is a real finding, not an oversight.
- Note **tenure at the company** for each director (e.g. two of nine go-forward
  directors joined five months before separation).
- Note **who reports to whom during the transition** — the employment agreement, not the
  org chart. An incoming CEO reporting to the *outgoing* CEO for months is a finding.

### 2. Find what each person has actually SAID — and record the silence
Search each name against their prior company and a topic. Look for interviews,
podcasts, conference talks, letters, trade-press quotes. Quote them.

**Most executives and directors have said nothing publicly, and that is a finding to
report, not a gap to pad.** Often half the roster has zero public intellectual
footprint. Say so plainly rather than inflating a bio into a view.

Best sources, in order: long-form podcast/interview transcripts; trade press (it asks
sharper questions than the business wires); conference keynotes; university lectures.

**Do not infer an opinion from a job title.** "Ran the oil & gas division" is not a view
on anything. If the search comes back empty, write "nothing public."

### 3. Read the ALLOCATION — who went where, and what they took
This is the pass that earns the skill. For any split, merger, or reorganisation:

- Which entity got the **operating** talent vs. the **caretaker** talent?
- Which got the **complete** C-suite and which is still recruiting?
- Where did the **CTO / chief scientist** go? Technology follows people. If you own the
  company for its pipeline and the pipeline's architect is leaving with the other half,
  you no longer own what you thought you owned.
- What is each **board built to do**? Score its composition: operators vs. retired
  cost-cutters vs. financial engineers vs. sector-native vs. generalist. A board of
  retired industrial executives with a value-investor chair is built to sell or to
  harvest margin, not to fund a decade of R&D. Say which, and what that implies.
- Where did the **incumbent CEO** put themself? A CEO who declines the entity that keeps
  the company's name is telling you something.

### 3b. ACQUISITIONS INVERT PASS 3 — ask who was put ON the broken thing

On a **split** you ask which half got the operators. On an **acquisition** the asset is
already known, so the questions become:

- **Who was put in charge of the acquired business, and how senior are they?** In one
  retail acquisition the buyer's Executive Chairman — its former long-time CEO —
  personally took operating control of the acquired chain.
- **Did the acquired company's CEO survive?** A CEO hired to run a turnaround and removed
  at close means the buyer rejected the existing turnaround thesis, not just the price.
- **Does the sitting CEO still control the whole P&L?** If a third of revenue reports to
  someone else, **an unusual governance structure is a sizing input, not a footnote.**
- **Read it BOTH ways and say both.** Founder-level intervention is **commitment** (the
  company owns the turnaround) *and* **admission** (the asset was worse than the deal
  assumed). Do not resolve this into one adjective.
- **Price the write-off against the purchase price.** E.g. up to $750M of clean-up and
  integration charges against a $2.4B purchase, $516M already recognised (~31% of the
  price inside twelve months), plus dozens of store closures in one quarter. **The
  charge ratio is the quantitative form of the personnel story** and the cleanest sizing
  input this pass produces.

**Look for a falsifiable claim by management about the acquired asset** — e.g. "it's a
supply problem, not a demand issue." That is the invalidation, stated for you: the next
earnings print can settle it. It is worth more than any bio on the page.

### 4. Check each principal's own track record — including how they left
For the CEO and CFO of the entity you would actually own:

- **How did they leave the last job?** "Retired to pursue other opportunities" is
  frequently a disagreement. Search the trade and national press, not the press release.
  A CEO who left a prior seat over a refused spin-off and is now running a spin-off is
  repeating one conviction, not making a fresh strategic read.
- **Have they run this kind of asset before?** A specialty-chemicals operator is a
  defensible fit for crop protection and a bad fit for seed genetics. Name the fit.
- **Do they own their own mistakes in public?** "I misread demand and over-built" is rarer
  and more valuable than a record of never being wrong.
- **Is there a health, legal, or availability disclosure?** Search "<name> retire",
  "<name> step down", "<name> lawsuit". **Report only what a filing or the company states,
  once, as an availability concentration risk — never as a character judgement, and never
  speculate beyond the filing.** The investment question is only whether a successor exists.
- **A departure under a governance cloud is an ASSET question, not a character one.**
  Report the documented facts once, then convert them into the one question that matters
  to the position (e.g. does this executive's key supplier relationship still work in the
  company's favour?). If that is unresolvable from public sources, say so rather than
  guessing.
- **First-time public-company CFO?** Count the firsts stacked in one seat. A first CFO
  role, at a first-time standalone company, with retained legacy liabilities and a first
  independent capital structure, is four firsts — a sizing input.

### 5. Verdict — and the rule that keeps it honest
Deliver: the strongest person on the roster, the weakest link, the single most material
personnel fact, and what the allocation implies about the company's own view of itself.

**Leadership quality NEVER overrides valuation or recognition.** A genuine CEO upgrade
does not make a consensus long at eighty times forward earnings with zero Sell ratings a
buy.

So this pass can:
- **Downgrade** a name that otherwise screened well (vacancies, an untested CFO on a
  liability-carrying balance sheet, a board built to harvest rather than build).
- **Explain** a de-rate that looked purely narrative.
- **Reveal** which half of a split holds the value, ahead of the market.

It cannot **promote** a name that failed on valuation or recognition. Say so explicitly
in the verdict so a later reader cannot mistake enthusiasm about people for a buy case.

## Output shape

Two rosters (person · record · what they've actually said, with "nothing public" where
true) -> the allocation read -> the principals' track records including how they left ->
verdict naming the strongest, the weakest link, and the material fact. Lead with the
allocation read; it is the part nobody else has done.

Not investment advice; a research method.
