# brand-direction-plates — five languages, one mark

The engine and its flags live in **`make-art`**. The delivery discipline, the ranking
rule and the two destinations live in **`image-riff`** — read that first; this skill
only records where a *brand direction spread* departs from it, and why.

## The inversion — this is the whole skill

`image-riff` holds the STEM (rendering language) constant and varies the SUBJECT,
because a base image supplies the language and you're exploring what to point it at.

A brand spread is the exact opposite. The client has one subject — their mark, their
trade — and no language yet. So:

```
LANGUAGE   varies, one complete visual world per plate   ← the deliverable
SUBJECT    varies with it (see vantage, below)
TAIL       invariant: geometry, type bed, negatives, brand invariants
```

**The brand invariants go in the TAIL, not the STEM.** For a business called
"Riverside Pegasus Automotive" (a worked example) that would be: *"The subject is a winged horse (Pegasus) and the world of
automobiles and machines — both must be unmistakable."* Put in the TAIL they hold
across five wildly different languages; put in each STEM they drift.

## The name is the entire brief

In a blind build the business name is all you get, and it is enough. Mine it for
three things and nothing else:

| from the name | example | what it becomes |
|---|---|---|
| the **mark** | Pegasus | the constant — in the TAIL, present in every plate |
| the **trade** | Automotive | the second TAIL invariant — machines must be legible |
| the **place** | Riverside | tone only; do not draw landmarks you have not verified |

Place is the one to hold loosely. A blind build has no licence to put a specific
bridge or skyline in a plate — get it wrong and the plate is worse than generic.

## Five languages, because five surfaces

Five is the right number, and not because it is a round one. Each plate should be
the best answer for a *different* surface, so the client can pick by use rather than
by taste. The five registers that spanned the space on the grounding run:

| register | what it claims | the surface it wins |
|---|---|---|
| **heritage sign** — enamel, hand-painted, screenprint | *we have been here* | avatar, signage, anything that must be a flat mark |
| **technical schematic** — blueprint, cutaway, exploded axonometric | *we understand the machine* | small print — card, invoice, one-colour |
| **photographic atmosphere** — night, weather, long exposure | *this is what it feels like* | website hero |
| **hand-cut craft** — woodcut, relief, letterpress, two inks | *honest work* | wall, merch, shirts |
| **airbrush swagger** — candy lacquer, chrome, metalflake | *we're the fun one* | social, anything that stops a thumb |

That grid is a starting deck, not scripture — swap a register when the trade wants
one it doesn't have. But keep the property that made it work: **each register claims
a different thing.** Five languages that all claim "premium" is one plate in five
costumes.

## Vantage still varies, or the spread collapses

`image-riff`'s vantage law survives intact here and is easy to forget once the
languages are doing the visible work. Five directions that all frame the mark the
same way crop identically and therefore do the same job. On the grounding run:
looking **up** at the underside · **down** a street at road level · flat-on with **no
horizon** · flat **side profile** · low **three-quarter** breaking the horizon.

## The type bed is technical — decide it yourself

Brand hero art carries a wordmark later. That is a surface fact, not a creative
choice, so it belongs in the invariant TAIL and you do not ask about it:

> Keep a calm, uncluttered region across the lower portion of the frame — low detail,
> low contrast, no focal element — where a wordmark will later be set.

Say *region*, not "lower third" — a fixed band contorts compositions that want to be
built the other way up, and the model negotiates a soft version cleanly. It still
loses sometimes (the woodcut plate filled its bottom with wrecked cars); note the
miss in the rank rather than re-generating over it.

## The engine returns the ARTEFACT, not the art

**The most expensive lesson of building these, and it can cost two of five plates.**
Any language named after a *physical object* comes back as a **photograph of that
object**:

- *"porcelain enamel sign art"* → a photo of a steel sign, with plate edge, rounded
  corners, mounting screw holes and enamel chips, on a wall.
- *"custom-van mural art"* → a photo of a van, with a door handle, panel seams and
  body reflections across the lower third.

Both are beautiful and neither is usable as a hero — a brand plate cannot be a
picture of somebody else's object. The TAIL negatives *"no border, no frame, no
margin"* do **not** prevent it; the model reads those as being about pale paper edges.

Two lines fix it, and both are needed:

1. In the TAIL: *"This is flat original artwork that fills the entire image — NOT a
   photograph of an object that has artwork on it."*
2. In that plate's STEM, **name the object's parts**, because a generic denial is
   ignored and a specific one isn't: *"There is no physical sign in this picture: no
   sign edge, no metal plate, no mounting holes, no screw holes, no rounded corner
   plate, no chipping, no wall behind it."*

**Before re-generating, try the crop.** An artefact photo is usually rescued by
cutting *inside* the object — the enamel plate's hero cut (`432,235 → 5072,2845` off
a 5504×3072 master) removed every screw hole and chip and left clean flat artwork.
That is a crop decision, which is your job anyway; a re-gen is $0.24 and a new
composition you may like less.

## The heritage-mark trap — check this before you ship

<EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>
A business whose name is a famous trademark's mascot will pull the engine straight
onto that trademark. **A *Pegasus* auto shop returned Mobil's flying red
horse** — cream and signal red, leaping, wings swept — on the one plate whose whole
job was to be a flat usable mark. Same trap waits on Lion, Shell, Ram, Falcon,
Thunderbird, Greyhound, and any animal that ever sold petrol.

This matters more here than in any other art run because **the client will use it as
their actual mark.** A hero plate that quotes a live trademark is not a style
reference; it is an infringement with the client's name under it.

So, every time:

1. **Name the risk in the README and in the reply.** Once, plainly, without hedging.
   A wrong mark shipped silently is the failure this rule exists to prevent.
2. **Steer the animal off it in the prompt** — change the *construction*, not just the
   palette. Angular art-deco facets and straight-edged planes instead of the flowing
   curve is what breaks the resemblance; a colour swap alone does not.
3. **Never present the drifted plate as the pick**, however good it is. Rank it where
   it lands and say what disqualifies it.
</EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>

## Ask one question, not two

`image-riff`'s second gate — *how far from the base* — is moot with no base, and the
spread itself is the answer to *how far apart*. Asking "which register?" would
collapse the deliverable before it exists. So ask exactly one:

**What surface are these plates for?** Emblem/mark (square, the small-size test
governs, see `image-riff`) · hero art / brand world (wide, type bed, no small-size
constraint) · both. Everything else you decide.

## Deliver

The destination from `image-riff`: a folder per business, e.g.
`art/<business-slug>/`, with a rank-table README and finals numbered by rank. Keep
generated art out of any personal photo library.

Two extra cuts a brand spread always needs, off each 16:9 master:

- `hero-1920/` — 1920×1080, the website hero.
- `og-1200/` — 1200×630, the social/OG card. Wider than 16:9 (1.905 vs 1.778), so
  keep full width and trim height; don't letterbox.

And rank **twice**: honestly against the brief, then one line per surface saying which
plate that surface wants. A rank is not a pick,
and on brand work the client is choosing by use, not by beauty.
