# Where a stranger reads you

## The axiom

**A stranger reads a message only where something vouches for the sender or obliges the
reader.** Cold email that opens on the sender has neither: no identity behind the From line,
no reason the reader owes a reply, and a spam filter trained on exactly this shape. Rank every
channel by what vouches.

| voucher | what it is | examples |
|---|---|---|
| **obligation** | the reader must or expects to answer strangers | records statutes, open job reqs, RFPs, public-comment windows, a "contact us" form on their own site, a directory they submitted to |
| **super user** | one person the buyer already pays or follows, who stands in front of MANY buyers | wedding planner -> couples; business broker -> sellers; CPA -> owners; insurance agent -> warehouses; association chair -> members |
| **person** | someone they trust said your name first, once | referral, mutual connection, ex-coworker, a vendor they already pay |
| **physical** | an object in their hands or a body at their door | a mailed piece, a walk-in, a room they chose to enter |
| **platform** | the platform shows who you are before they open it | LinkedIn profile + mutuals, a registry or marketplace listing, a community they read |
| **their own record** | the first two sentences quote something the reader produced in public, so the message is visibly about them alone | their listing, a filing, board minutes, a permit, their own website's words. The only voucher that works inside a cold email |
| **none** | nothing vouches | a cold email, SMS or DM that opens on you |

## Obligation inboxes — where reading strangers is the job

Some inboxes get read because reading them is someone's job: a records office, a business's
quote or booking form, a dataset catalog's PR queue, a small-business resource desk.

**The scope test is the whole rule.** The obligation binds only when what you submit is *the
thing that inbox exists to receive*. A records request in a records inbox gets an answer. A
pitch in a "request a quote" form gets read once, resented, and marked as spam.

| inbox | what it exists for | what to submit |
|---|---|---|
| a business's quote / booking / "tell us about your project" form | demand | **actual demand** — e.g. a list of jobs they could win, from public records, on a page made for them |
| a records office | requests | a records request (see `records-request`) |
| a dataset catalog (an awesome-list PR, Hugging Face, Kaggle, a state geospatial portal) | datasets | the dataset itself |
| a resource desk (business librarian, SBDC advisor, city economic-development office) | free resources for businesses | your tool as a free resource; each desk fronts many businesses, so it's also a super user |

Rules for forms:
- **Hand them demand nobody has sold yet**, never their own trade's already-won work (a roof
  permit to a roofer is a job someone already won; new roofs with no solar permit are leads
  for a solar installer).
- **Never invent a field.** If a form requires a job address you don't have, skip it — making
  one up is posing as a customer.
- **Skip any form that says no solicitation or vendors.** Embedded form tools hide that text
  from a plain HTML read; check the live form.
- **A CAPTCHA gets a human click, not a solver.** Fill it, hand it over for the tick and submit.
- **A form counts as sent only on the site's confirmation** (thank-you page, success message,
  confirmation email). A submit click alone is "unconfirmed"; never resend.

## The super user — sell through whoever already holds the trust

A wedding photographer's first instinct is to sell to couples: every couple is a stranger, buys
once, and has to be won from zero. The planner has already been chosen by dozens of couples a
year, is paid to recommend vendors, and looks better when the photos are good. **One trust
relationship with the planner is worth forty with couples, and it renews itself.**

**A super user is qualified by four facts, all of which must hold:**
1. The buyer already pays them, or takes their advice on an adjacent purchase.
2. They stand in front of many buyers — dozens a year, not one.
3. They gain when your thing works: their client's outcome, a fee, or credit. If they gain
   nothing, they're a one-time referral, not a channel.
4. They are few enough to reach by person, physical, or platform.

**Traps.** A super user who sells the same thing is a competitor. A hub with no relationship to
the buyer (a subreddit, a directory) is a platform, not a super user. And the super user's
clients must be YOUR buyer class.

## Rules, each with its why

1. **Email works as a first touch when it opens on the reader's own record.** What fails is the
   message that leads with you, on any channel. In one founder's ledger, well over a thousand
   sends that opened on the sender returned essentially no positive replies, while every single
   yes — on email, LinkedIn or a form — opened on something the reader had produced in public.
2. **Before choosing a channel to the buyer, name the buyer's super users**, by fan-out. Reaching
   buyers directly needs a reason. Trust is the scarce input; a super user divides it by their
   fan-out and makes it recur.
3. **Before writing, list where this buyer (or their super user) already accepts strangers** —
   job reqs, RFPs, statutes, their contact form, their association, the registry they search.
   Pick the strongest voucher. An obliged reader answers the same facts that get silence by
   cold email.
4. **Physical beats digital at mid-market size.** A mailed piece or a walk-in reaches the owner
   of a 50-person business in one hop.
5. **One channel per buyer class per fortnight, three touches, reply kinds logged.** Silence is
   a finding only when the channel got its three touches — and a fair sample: roughly 50
   delivered recipients across 3+ segments. Fewer is a probe, never a verdict.
6. **Carry a credential on every touch.** A public, checkable story (a newsletter post, a
   portfolio) is what vouches when no person does.
7. **The super user gets an offer of their own**, not the buyer's pitch forwarded. Their gain
   goes in the first line.
8. **A human reads every draft and presses send.** A platform DM is contact. Money (ads, paid
   messaging credits, referral fees) is a separate decision.
9. **Through an obligation inbox, send only what that inbox is for.** The obligation disappears
   the moment the reader sees an out-of-scope pitch.
10. **When the goal is visibility, the gift IS the pitch.** One line on what you found, the
    link, "free, no reply needed". No offer.
11. **The first two sentences quote the reader's own public record. Then ask for nothing, or
    only for what they owe.** Their post, filing, minutes, permit, menu, listing — not their
    industry or "businesses like yours". If no such record exists for a named person, that row
    is not ready to send. A record has to be looked up, which is what proves the message is
    theirs alone.
12. **A paid product's intro sells the click, never gives the product away.** Point at the free
    preview and state the price in one line ("Ten are open to poke at, no login: <link>. The
    full list is $100, once."). No comp passwords or "here's yours free" unless the batch is
    explicitly a gift. **Confirm sale vs. visibility before writing a batch**; an instruction
    about the offer expires when the product's price changes.
13. **A batch is a probe until it shows clicks; read visits at day 3.** Zero visits at day 3
    means the batch didn't arrive or wasn't opened: a pipe problem that copy edits won't fix.
    Bulk-provider batches can draw zero clicks where the same number of one-to-one sends draw
    dozens of visitors.
14. **Every link carries the recipient in the PATH (`/for/<token>/`), never `?id=` or `#x`.**
    Web analytics commonly drop the query and hash, so query tokens can't tell you who opened
    what.
15. **For a paid tool, a checkout started and abandoned is the price talking, not the channel.**
    Change one thing about the offer (price, what it buys, the preview) before sending more
    people at it.
16. **Don't text prospects from a personal phone number.** A prospect who has it can reach that
    person there forever. Use a business channel (a marketplace relay, a business page message,
    their contact form), or hold the row.

## Procedure

1. Name the buyer class and the one offer.
2. Name the super users: who is already paid or followed on an adjacent decision, and how many
   buyers does each front? Check the four facts. If one qualifies, they are who you reach.
3. Fill the voucher table for whoever you're reaching (obligation -> super user -> person ->
   physical -> platform), citing the source for each row.
4. Choose the strongest available voucher. Email is fine as touch one when line one is the
   reader's own record.
5. Build the list with a NAMED person **and that person's own public record**. A row with a name
   and no record is not sendable.
6. **Pick the pipe by size.** One-to-one from your own mailbox, rate-capped (see
   `zoho-send-cap`), is the default; use a bulk provider only for a batch that has already shown
   clicks.
7. At day 3, read visits by day and by `/for/<token>/` path (see `site-analytics`), then log
   every touch and reply kind. At day 60, read one table per channel, super users counted as
   their own channel.
